Google Suncatcher: AI Data Centers in Space

02 Nov 2026 Source: The New York Times Space · AI · EO

The most audacious infrastructure proposal of the AI era is not on Earth. Google's Project Suncatcher, as reported by The New York Times, is a step toward putting AI data centers in space — compute racks reimagined as satellites, drinking unfiltered sunlight far above weather, clouds and land-use disputes.

The design Google's researchers published is refreshingly specific. Instead of a monolithic orbital station, Suncatcher imagines modular clusters of 81 small satellites flying in tight formation around 650 kilometers up — a kilometer-scale array of interconnected compute nodes, closer and more coordinated than any constellation ever flown. The first prototype, a fridge-size satellite built with Planet Labs and launched in October, carries four TPUs and runs in short bursts while the team learns the hard constraints.

And the constraints are the story. Radiation flips bits in ways data centers never worry about; Google radiation-tested its TPUs on the ground and in orbit before trusting them. Cooling, the dominant cost of terrestrial AI, must work in vacuum where there is no air to carry heat away. And every joule must be rationed: the prototype operates one TPU at a time, fifteen minutes per pass, because power is the binding budget.

The economics that make it worth attempting: solar yield is five to seven times higher without an atmosphere, panels never see clouds, and the land is free. If launch costs keep falling, compute could follow the same trajectory as imaging — born on the ground, then migrating to orbit once the orbit becomes cheaper.

The prototype has already answered its first prompt from orbit. The distance between a haiku and a hyperscale orbital data center is enormous — but so was the distance between Sputnik and Starlink. The era of computing where the sun actually lives has formally begun.
