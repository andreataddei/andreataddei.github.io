The Space Brolly Blooms in Orbit

15 May 2025 Source: Space.com EO · Space

Two weeks after launch, the moment of truth for ESA's forest-carbon mission arrived: Biomass deployed its 12-meter radar antenna in orbit, unfolding like a flower into the configuration that lets it see through tropical canopies.

Large deployable structures are the aristocrats of spacecraft engineering. The antenna — a reflector suspended on a boom — must fold into a rocket fairing, survive launch vibration, then deploy in vacuum with millimeter precision. There is no second attempt, and the sequence is rehearsed for years on gravity-offset rigs on the ground.

The scale is unusual for a European science satellite. Larger than the spacecraft bus itself, the reflector dominates every design decision: attitude control must compensate for its inertia, thermal control must handle a structure that flexes as it passes in and out of sunlight.

The reward for the engineering discipline is scientific reach. P-band radar at that aperture can penetrate forest canopy and measure biomass directly — data no other civilian mission can gather.

Deployment day is the ritual of the industry: years of work compressed into a few hours of telemetry that either says "nominal" repeatedly or does not. This one did. The forest-carbon data that climate negotiators need now has its instrument open for business.

The deployment also validated Europe's quieter competence: large space structures. Between Biomass's reflector and the antenna farms of the coming expansion missions, ESA is quietly accumulating one of the industry's deepest playbooks on deployable mechanics — an export product in itself as commercial constellations begin demanding ever-larger folded hardware.
