NASA's Earth Observation Archive Passes 100 Petabytes

12 Jan 2026 Source: Brookings Space · EO · AI

NASA's Earth Observation System Data and Information System has quietly passed a milestone that says a lot about where the space sector is going: its archive has already hit 100 petabytes, and it is expected to reach 320 petabytes in the years ahead. To put that in perspective: one petabyte is roughly a million high-resolution photos. We are no longer data-poor about our planet — we are drowning in it, on purpose.

The growth is driven by two curves crossing. Mega-constellations numbering in the thousands are shrinking revisit times from days to hours, while instrument resolution keeps climbing. Every new satellite multiplies the data rate of every existing pipeline downstream. The bottleneck has moved: it is no longer collecting pixels, it is turning pixels into decisions.

That is why the analysts at Brookings frame the real prize as "time-to-insight." A flood map that arrives two days late is an autopsy; the same map arriving two hours early is an evacuation. AI is the technology that collapses that gap, because petabyte-scale streams cannot be read by humans at all. Models that classify land cover, detect vessels, track deforestation or estimate emissions are effectively the readers of an archive nobody could otherwise open.

The opportunity and the risk arrive together, as they usually do. The same pipelines that accelerate climate response also concentrate power: whoever controls the archive and the models controls the insight. Governance has to catch up with capability — data access rules, model transparency, and sovereignty over imagery of your own territory.

The lesson for anyone building in this space is simple. The satellites are the easy part now. The value is in the layer that asks the archive a question and gets an answer in time to matter.
