Real-Time Earth Observation: From Hours to Minutes

25 May 2026 Source: IEEE Spectrum EO · AI

Buried in IEEE Spectrum's reporting on Planet Labs' orbital AI is the number that should reframe how everyone thinks about Earth observation: real-time AI in space can turn raw satellite data into urgent wildfire and ship alerts, shrinking response times from hours to minutes. Not better pictures — faster answers.

To understand why this is a discontinuity, walk the old pipeline. A satellite captures an image, stores it, waits for a ground-station pass, downlinks raw data, transfers it to a processing center, queues it behind every other image of the day, gets processed, gets analyzed, gets formatted, gets delivered. Each step adds minutes to hours, and every step exists because compute used to be too heavy to fly. The event does not wait for the pipeline, which is why disaster response has historically been an exercise in arriving accurately but late.

Move the first stages of that pipeline into orbit and the physics changes. Detection happens in the same revolution as imaging; what crosses the downlink is not a scene but an answer — coordinates, classifications, confidence — a few hundred bytes instead of a few hundred megabytes. Alert latency drops to the time it takes the spacecraft to see the event and the radio to reach a ground station.

The dual-use logic is what makes this durable. The same onboard models that flag a fire perimeter for a civil-protection agency count ships for a port authority, track changes for an insurer, and cue high-resolution tasking for a defense user. One capability, many budgets — which is how infrastructure gets funded.

And the trajectory points further in: from detecting known objects to asking onboard models arbitrary questions about a scene. The satellite stops being a camera and becomes a field analyst with a planetary commute. When answers arrive in minutes, monitoring becomes something new — not a record of what happened, but an instrument for what happens next.
