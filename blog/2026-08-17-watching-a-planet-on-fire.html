Watching a Planet on Fire

17 Aug 2026 Source: ESA EO · Climate

The summer of 2026 wrote its own headline. Since 1 May, the Copernicus Emergency Management Service was activated more than 30 times to provide data and images on wildfires — in Croatia and across the Mediterranean, in a season of high temperatures, drought and extreme heat that ESA's own monitoring describes as exceptional.

What the article documents is emergency observation working as a system, with each instrument playing its part. Sentinel-2's optical imagery maps burn scars and active fronts when skies are clear. Radar sees through the smoke when they are not. And Sentinel-5P measures the trace gases and aerosols that fires emit in large quantities — turning regional air quality from a ground-level guess into an orbit-tracked variable, downwind of the flames and across borders.

Thirty activations in a season is not a record to celebrate, but it is a record of capability: within hours of a request, civil-protection authorities receive delineation maps, damage grading and reference layers for coordination — products that used to require days of aerial surveying. The activation count is effectively a count of crises where decisions were made with current, accurate maps instead of assumptions.

ESA ties this explicitly to its Space for Our Climate framing, and the connection is not rhetorical. Fire is where the climate crisis becomes immediately operational: drought conditions set the fuel load, extreme heat provides the ignition weather, and response speed is the only variable fully in human hands. Satellites compress that variable.

The honest summary of a hard season: the planet burned more, and we saw it better. Both facts are true at once, and the second is why the damage was not worse. Continuous, open, multi-sensor observation is ceasing to be a scientific luxury — it is becoming the standard equipment of a civilization that has run out of weather to be surprised by.
