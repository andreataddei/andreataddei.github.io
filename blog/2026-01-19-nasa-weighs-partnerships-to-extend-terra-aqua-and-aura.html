NASA Weighs Partnerships to Extend Terra, Aqua and Aura

19 Jan 2026 Source: Washington Technology Space · EO · Policy

Some of the most important satellites in climate science are also among the oldest. NASA is now exploring public-private partnerships to extend the data collection missions of Terra, Aqua and Aura — three flagship Earth observation spacecraft that have been flying since the early 2000s and are well past their design lives.

The satellites can still deliver valuable data on the atmosphere, land and oceans. Their feeds feed weather forecasting, air quality monitoring and disaster response. A multi-decade record from the same instruments is exactly what makes climate trends statistically meaningful: you cannot measure change without an unbroken baseline. Decommissioning a healthy sensor because the paperwork expired would be a self-inflicted wound on science.

The partnership structures NASA is considering read like a menu for the whole sector. Fully reimbursable models, where a partner pays NASA to run everything. Non-reimbursable models, where the partner takes over operations and costs. Hybrid arrangements that split responsibility. Each shifts a different slice of risk and cost between public and private hands.

This matters beyond one agency. It is a live experiment in whether commercial operators can be trusted with continuity of record — the property that makes Earth observation data valuable in the first place. A weather startup can tolerate a gap; a 30-year climate time series cannot.

There is also a broader signal here for the private space boom: the most valuable space assets are not the newest constellations but the longest-running datasets. If industry learns to be a custodian of continuity rather than only a builder of novelty, the public-private model works. If not, we will relearn, gap by gap, why the quiet satellites nobody talks about were the ones we could least afford to lose.
