Sentinel-1D and 5A Return First Images

26 Nov 2025 Source: European Commission EO · Space

The commissioning phase is the quiet ritual after every launch, and in late November it produced its result: Sentinel-1D and Sentinel-5A returned their first images — one radar portrait of the ground, one spectral scan of the air, marking both missions as healthy in orbit.

First light images are more than ceremony. They are the first data point in a calibration campaign that decides whether a spacecraft can deliver on its promises: does the radar's geometry match the archive, do the spectrometer's bands resolve the trace gases they were tuned for, does everything hold through a full orbit's thermal cycle?

That the two arrive together is the architecture speaking. Sentinel-1D extends a decade-old radar record whose calibration must match its predecessors almost exactly; Sentinel-5A opens a new atmospheric record whose calibration sets its own standard. Continuity and novelty, shipped in the same season.

The engineering substance hides in the word "first": geolocation verified against ground truth, instrument modes exercised one by one, products validated against known targets before the data enters the operational archive.

When these images reach full service quality, they join an open record with no subscription, no gatekeeping — the daily, growing portrait of the planet that anyone with an API key can query. Another two instruments have just joined the world's largest open data acquisition.

For anyone building on Copernicus data, the calendar matters too: with both instruments in calibration, full service data typically follows within months — meaning the 2026 analysis season starts with a complete radar pair and a new atmospheric spectrometer under the tree.
