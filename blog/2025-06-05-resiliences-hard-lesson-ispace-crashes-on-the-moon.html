Resilience's Hard Lesson: ispace Crashes on the Moon

05 Jun 2025 Source: Space.com Space

Landing on the Moon punished ispace for the second time. The company's Resilience lander went dark during final descent and struck the surface too fast, in what the company called a hard landing — the end of its second mission in three years.

The telemetry cut out near the moment of truth: roughly 100 meters above the surface, during the phase where the vehicle must transition from orbital velocities to a survivable touchdown. Later analysis pointed at laser range-finding errors — the spacecraft did not know how high it was, and its engine throttled accordingly.

The bitter symmetry: the first mission, in 2023, was also lost to altitude-estimation errors during descent. Two different vehicles, one underlying truth — the last hundred meters of a lunar landing are a sensor-fusion problem with no margin for noise.

The wider picture is less bleak than the scoreboard. In the same season, Firefly's Blue Ghost landed gently and Intuitive Machines' Athena met its own hard ending. Private lunar delivery is iterating in public, exactly as NASA's CLPS model intended: several providers, several attempts, a steeper collective learning curve.

Every crash teaches something no simulation can. Resilience's final data stream — altitude, velocity, engine commands — is already a gift to the next flight computer. The Moon is being mapped by failures as much as landings right now.

ispace's response defined the company too: public telemetry analysis within days, root cause published, and a stated commitment to refly. In a market where failure is now expected, the differentiator is how visibly and how quickly a provider metabolizes it into the next design review.
