Biomass: The Forest-Carbon Satellite Launches

29 Apr 2025 Source: UN-SPIDER EO · Space

Europe's strangest-looking satellite left Earth in late April: ESA's Biomass launched from Kourou on a Vega-C rocket, carrying a radar that peers through forest canopy to weigh the wood beneath.

The instrument is a 12-meter P-band antenna — a wavelength around 70 centimeters, long enough to bounce off trunks and branches instead of leaves. No civilian satellite had flown P-band before: the band is normally reserved for military radar, and securing it required international frequency coordination measured in decades.

The science target is the carbon cycle. Tropical forests store and swap enormous amounts of carbon, but their biomass has been estimated with error bars larger than the emissions of entire industrial sectors. Biomass turns those estimates into measurements, across the tropics, repeatedly, for five years.

It is climate policy infrastructure as much as science. Carbon markets, deforestation treaties and national inventories all depend on forest-carbon numbers being trustworthy — the difference between a forest being worth more standing or cleared.

Watch the deployment sequence in the weeks after launch: the satellite unfolds like a flower into a shape rarely seen in orbit. Engineering originality in service of measurement rigor — the most European of space missions, and the quiet workhorse kind that eventually changes what the world's climate numbers actually mean.

The mission completes a division of labor the EO world has awaited for a decade: optical missions map where forests are, radar missions watch how they change, and Biomass alone answers how much they weigh — the number the entire carbon-accounting edifice ultimately rests on.
