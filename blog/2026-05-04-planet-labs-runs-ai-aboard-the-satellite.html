Planet Labs Runs AI Aboard the Satellite

4 May 2026 Source: IEEE Spectrum EO · AI

AI image processing aboard satellites has been a goal of the Earth observation industry for years. Now it has finally been achieved: Planet Labs released an image captured by its Pelican-4 multispectral satellite showing an airport in Alice Springs, Australia — with more than a dozen aircraft on the tarmac, each highlighted in a neat green box, identified by an AI model running aboard the spacecraft.

The detail that should impress any engineer is the timeline: Planet's engineers worked eighteen months to achieve this. Running inference in orbit is not a software patch. Radiation-tolerant compute is scarce, power is rationed by the solar bus, thermal budgets punish sustained number-crunching, and every model must be compressed, quantized and validated against the exact sensor it will fly with. The green boxes in that image represent a solution to all four constraints simultaneously.

The economics drive the design. Planet's low-cost space cameras scan the entire surface of Earth multiple times a day at around 5 meters of resolution, while the larger Pelican fleet adds sharper tasking. A constellation imaging the whole planet daily cannot downlink everything — the business only closes if the spacecraft decides what is worth sending. Onboard AI is what turns a firehose into a queue of detections: ships, aircraft, fires, changes, anomalies.

The company frames it as unlocking "real-time planetary intelligence," and the phrase is precise. Detection at the sensor collapses the latency between event and alert from the scale of downlink scheduling to the scale of an orbit.

This is also a hiring signal disguised as a news item: the scarce skill is knowing how to make a good model small enough to survive launch and valuable enough to justify the effort. That intersection — modern AI engineering applied under aerospace constraints — is exactly where the industry is heading, and where the interesting work will be for years.
