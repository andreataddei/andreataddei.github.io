Sentinel-2 Explores Night Vision

26 Jan 2026 Source: ESA EO · Copernicus

After more than ten years in orbit, the first Copernicus Sentinel-2 satellite is still finding new ways to contribute to Earth observation. With Sentinel-2B and 2C now carrying the core daytime imaging task, ESA is repurposing the veteran spacecraft to explore a capability it was never designed for: seeing at night.

Sentinel-2A was launched in 2015 and marked a milestone by combining a high-resolution multispectral optical imager with a 290 km-wide swath — camera-like images of the planet's surface, refreshed constantly, free for everyone. A decade later, that same instrument is being pointed at the dark side of the day.

Night acquisitions are not a gimmick. A large share of what matters about human activity happens between dusk and dawn: gas flares, fishing fleets, fires, power outages, illegal mining, migration of light pollution. Night lights are one of the most honest datasets in development economics — they show where energy actually reaches, not where reports claim it does. Teaching an aging optical satellite to make useful nighttime measurements extends a public dataset that policymakers, researchers and journalists already rely on.

There is an engineering lesson here that applies far beyond Copernicus. Instruments outlive their mission definitions. A satellite designed in the 2000s is answering questions from the 2020s because the underlying hardware was overbuilt and the software is updatable. Longevity is a feature you design in on day one — in rad-hardened components, in margin on the power bus, in the ability to re-task the imaging chain from the ground.

The practical payoff: continuity with creativity. The same pixels, a new clock. Ten years on, Sentinel-2A is proof that the most sustainable space strategy is to keep good instruments working and keep asking what else they can see.
